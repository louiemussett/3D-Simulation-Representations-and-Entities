# Living Laboratory — Simulation Guide

## What is this simulation?

**Living Laboratory is a generational animal-society simulation.** You observe a world in which every animal has its own body, incomplete knowledge, memories, relationships, preferences and history.

Animals do not merely wander until a need becomes urgent. They perceive what is around them, remember what they previously encountered, communicate with others, form relationships, coordinate in groups and attempt to satisfy several interdependent needs. Their choices can produce families, pairs, packs, herds, pregnancy-support networks, leadership changes, conflict, dispersal and new generations.

There is no player character and no single winning strategy. The purpose is to watch the world develop, select an animal when something interesting happens and use the Laboratory to uncover why it happened.

> **Every visible animal is embedded in an unseen web of physiology, memory, family, preference, communication and social organisation—and the Laboratory lets you uncover it.**

The simulation is experimental. Its behaviour is generated by rules and seeded variation; it is not a validated scientific model and does not guarantee a stable ecosystem.

## How to learn by watching

The interface presents the simulation at three depths.

### 1. What is happening now?

Select an animal for a short, immediate explanation:

- what it is doing;
- why that action currently makes sense to it;
- where it intends to go;
- which private thought, visible expression or outward signal is active;
- which group it belongs to and what that group is trying to accomplish.

This explanation is deliberately brief. It answers the observational question without exposing every internal number.

### 2. How does this mechanic work?

Open the contextual guide from the selected animal or a discovery event. This explains the relevant system—such as thirst planning, herd defence, mate choice or leadership—without requiring you to read the whole encyclopedia.

### 3. What are all the underlying rules?

Open the complete Laboratory encyclopedia. This is where detailed physiology, evidence, memories, ancestry, relationships, group history, reproductive state and decision traces belong.

The world teaches through discoveries. A rare or important event can link directly to the appropriate guide entry:

> **Leadership changed**  
> H12 replaced H4 as movement leader after H4 became exhausted and H12 proved better suited to the group’s water goal.  
> **Learn how leadership changes**

## The core-mechanics map

An animal’s behaviour is produced by connected systems rather than one fixed priority ladder.

```text
Environment
   ↓ creates opportunities, costs and hazards
Perception → Evidence → Memory and communication
   ↓                         ↓
Physiology ← Need plans ← Estimated routes and outcomes
   ↓                         ↓
Capabilities → Candidate actions → Decision
                                  ↓
             Movement, interaction and consequences
                                  ↓
          New memories, relationships and group history
```

A need may depend on another need. A thirsty animal far from water may first need to recover enough endurance to travel. A hungry carnivore may need hydration, rest, prey evidence and a viable approach before hunting. The highest need is therefore not always the next visible action.

This is the most important principle for interpreting the simulation:

> **An action is a step inside a plan, not necessarily the direct satisfaction of the animal’s strongest need.**

## 1. Individual life

Every animal is an individual rather than an interchangeable unit of its species.

Its state includes age, sex, maturity, health, recoverable health, lasting injury, fat reserves, stomach fullness, hydration, sustainable endurance, sprint reserve, emergency exertion, temperature stress and reproductive condition. Traits influence aggression, sociability, care, resource skill, movement, perception and leadership suitability.

### Species and physiology

**Valley Grazers** eat vegetation and generally gain safety from nearby compatible animals. Their lives revolve around forage quality, accessible water, vigilance, caregiving and collective threat response.

**Ridge Hunters** eat prey and carcasses. They depend on prey evidence, tracking, stalking, pursuit condition, attack opportunity and recovery. A hungry hunter may still suppress a hunt if dehydration, fatigue, injury, pregnancy or poor odds make the attempt unsafe.

Hydration and feeding are not identical meters. Stomach contents provide immediate food, fat supports longer-term reserves and hydration has progressive consequences for movement, perception, recovery and survival. Species differ in how quickly these pressures matter and in how they acquire food.

### Interdependent needs

The simulation does not use a simple “largest number wins” hierarchy. It builds feasible routes toward need satisfaction.

Examples:

```text
Thirst
└── locate or remember water
    └── retain enough endurance for the journey
        └── reach an accessible shoreline
            └── face and contact the water
                └── drink

Carnivore hunger
└── preserve sufficient hydration and condition
    └── find current or remembered prey evidence
        └── evaluate risk and likely return
            └── track, stalk or chase
                └── attack, recover and feed
```

This explains why an animal may rest while thirsty, travel while not yet critically hungry or abandon a hunt despite visible prey.

## 2. Life stages

Animals pass through dependent young, juvenile, young-adult, adult and old stages. Their bodies, capabilities, social expectations and risks change with age.

- **Dependent young** rely on caregivers and cannot use the complete adult behavioural repertoire.
- **Juveniles** explore more actively, may sprint impulsively and can fall behind moving groups.
- **Young adults** gain reproductive and social independence and may disperse or seek new relationships.
- **Adults** have the broadest set of reproductive, caregiving, leadership and conflict behaviours.
- **Old animals** carry long histories and may become grandparents or more distant ancestors, but declining condition can alter travel and leadership.

A young animal that cannot keep pace can issue a wait-up or contact call. Parents, relatives, caregivers or other caring adults may slow, return or help—but patience, trust, urgency and group goals can also produce separation or abandonment.

---

## 3. Memory and perception

Animals do not possess a perfect map. They act from incomplete evidence.

They can see, hear, smell, receive signals and remember locations or individuals. Evidence has age, confidence, uncertainty and provenance. A remembered water region is not the same as a currently visible shoreline; a warning call is not proof that the receiver knows a predator’s exact position.

### Memory timescales

- **Short-term observations** retain recent sights, sounds, scents and interactions.
- **Strategic resource memory** supports later travel toward water, food, prey or carcasses.
- **Social memory** records trust, conflict, care, threats and individual encounters.
- **Group history** records losses, departures, leadership succession and important collective events.
- **Long-term kinship history** preserves ancestry and descendants across generations.

Memories can become stale or disproven. Resource plans should search around uncertain locations, strengthen exact contacts that succeed and reduce confidence in failed destinations.

### Fog of knowledge

Diagnostic views distinguish the true world from what a selected animal knows. Visible entities can be represented clearly, remembered ones approximately and unknown regions as fog. This makes it possible to understand a poor decision without granting the animal knowledge it never acquired.

---

## 4. Relationships and social networks

Proximity alone does not make every encounter friendly. Each animal has personal space and can respond when another individual enters it.

Responses range from ignoring or orienting toward the visitor to greeting, signalling, cooperating, courting, defending, disputing or attacking. Species, age, sex, familiarity, kinship, aggression, fear, condition and current goals all affect the outcome.

Social memory allows reactions to become individual-specific. An animal may remember a trusted caregiver, a helpful companion, an unreliable leader, a recurring rival or a predator that previously injured it. Pregnancy can increase the value of a dependable social network, while repeated conflict can create pressure to leave.

The social network is therefore not identical to current group membership. An animal can trust individuals outside its group, dislike a nearby member or retain a bond after separation.

---

## 5. Groups and societies

The simulation distinguishes ordinary groups from larger proximity-based organisations.

Ordinary groups may include:

- a bonded pair;
- a family with dependent offspring;
- a female social group;
- a male group;
- a reproductive association;
- a hunting party or pack.

Several nearby groups and individuals moving with compatible purpose can form a larger **meta-group**, such as a herd. A herd is not merely a large family: it is a temporary coordination structure created by proximity, direction, shared risk and collective purpose.

### Group goals

Groups pursue goals that can change with their members’ circumstances. Current goals include protection, pregnancy support, caregiving, water acquisition, foraging, hunting, carcass acquisition, mating and travel.

A group goal competes with personal needs rather than erasing them. An animal may follow the leader, attempt to influence the group, temporarily attend to an emergency or deliberately depart if membership becomes harmful.

---

## 6. Leadership and departure

Leadership is based on suitability for the group’s current problem, not age alone.

Older adults are often stable candidates, but a subadult—and in rare circumstances a juvenile—can lead if its knowledge, resource skill, communication, relationships or condition makes it substantially better suited. Water knowledge matters during a water goal; care and trust matter during pregnancy support; tracking and condition matter during a hunt.

Leadership does not change with every small fluctuation. Tenure and a meaningful advantage protect group stability. Replacement becomes more likely when the leader is impaired, loses trust or is clearly outperformed for the current goal.

Animals may leave because of maturity, mate seeking, crowding, resource competition, leader distrust, goal disagreement, personal emergency or repeated conflict. Departure can be temporary or permanent. A cooldown prevents an animal from instantly rejoining the same group and erasing the decision.

Group members remember departures and losses. A reduced group may seek companions, increase caregiving or reproductive pressure, or undergo leadership succession.

---

## 7. Courtship and mating systems

Reproduction begins with eligibility, opportunity and preference—not automatic pairing.

Mature animals evaluate condition, fertility, familiarity, social compatibility and remembered mate quality. Courtship is a visible interaction with possible acceptance or rejection. Successful mating proceeds through courtship, acceptance, mating and conception resolution; conception is not guaranteed merely because mating occurred.

Reproductive associations can emerge from repeated preferences and social organisation. When a reproductive group forms, the guide should explain the actual membership, bonds and leadership that produced it rather than presenting a special label as a scripted event.

Hearts are reserved for courtship or pair affection. Caregiving, dependency and family contact use their own signals so reproductive intent is not confused with parental care.

---

## 8. Pregnancy and caregiving

Pregnancy changes both physiology and planning.

A pregnant female must protect hydration, nutrition, fat reserves, temperature, endurance and safety while gestation progresses. Heavy pregnancy can make unnecessary hunting, sprinting or long-distance travel poor choices. The animal may remain near reliable food and water, seek trusted companions or influence its group toward pregnancy support.

Other animals do not receive magical knowledge of pregnancy. They can become aware through observation, familiarity and communication; once aware, care affinity, kinship and trust affect whether they offer protection or companionship.

After birth, offspring pass through dependency and later independence. Care can come from a mother, known caregivers or surviving adult relatives. Grandparents and more distant ancestors may matter when parents die or families reorganise. Caregivers can disagree, fight, withdraw care or abandon a dependent; these events become part of social and family memory.

---

## 9. Predation and threat response

Predation is a sequence rather than a single attack command.

A hunter can patrol with purpose, remember prey density and direction, evaluate potential calories, track evidence, stalk, chase, strike, recover, guard a carcass or abandon a poor hunt. Condition and risk matter throughout. Visible prey does not guarantee attack if the hunter is exhausted, dehydrated, injured, pregnant, recently unsuccessful or facing strong herd defence.

Herbivores can detect danger, warn others, flee, seek herd safety, shelter dependents, mob a predator or counterattack when numbers, confidence and protective motivation make resistance plausible. A young or isolated predator may still be treated as a threat, ignored, displaced or attacked depending on the herbivore’s evidence and circumstances.

Threat knowledge persists. Animals can remember where a predator was last seen, who attacked them and the direction from which danger arrived. Group members can communicate warnings without transmitting perfect certainty.

---

## 10. Seasons, weather and ecology

The landscape is an active participant in every life history.

Terrain affects routes, visibility and access. Rivers, lakes and small water sources shape movement. Vegetation differs in nutritional value and regrowth. Rain, temperature, storms, wind and seasonal change alter water, plant growth, thermal stress and resource distribution.

Carcasses are temporary ecological resources. They can be discovered, remembered, claimed, contested, eaten, scavenged and eventually decay into skeletons before removal.

There is no invisible controller that guarantees equilibrium. Population change emerges from births, deaths, local food and water, predation, condition, reproduction and movement. A world may flourish, fluctuate or collapse.

---

## 11. Generations and family trees

Birth creates more than a new population counter. It creates ancestry.

Animals retain parents, offspring, caregivers and multigenerational relationships. If they live long enough, individuals can become grandparents, great-grandparents and more distant ancestors. Descendants can enter groups containing older relatives, inherit social circumstances and respond to deaths or separations that reshape the family.

The family tree records biological descent; social care records who actually helped raise or protect an animal. These histories can overlap, but they are not assumed to be identical.

Across several generations, the visible population becomes the result of accumulated courtship, preference, survival, caregiving, dispersal, conflict and environmental opportunity.

## Questions to ask when observing

When an animal’s behaviour looks surprising, ask:

1. **What does it know?** Is the resource or threat visible, remembered, communicated or merely inferred?
2. **What can it currently do?** Check age, injury, hydration, endurance, pregnancy and terrain.
3. **What plan is it following?** The next action may satisfy a prerequisite rather than the final need.
4. **Who matters to it?** Look at relatives, caregivers, trusted companions, rivals, threats and mates.
5. **What is its group trying to do?** Personal and group goals may align, compete or cause departure.
6. **What recently changed?** Weather, loss, injury, birth, leadership, communication and stale memory can redirect behaviour.

The simulation becomes easiest to understand when you follow one animal over time. Watch what it perceives, pause when its behaviour changes, inspect the immediate explanation and then open the relevant Laboratory entry. The story is not only the action on screen; it is the history that made that action reasonable to that particular animal.
