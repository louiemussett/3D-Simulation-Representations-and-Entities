Yes. At the actual **default, unchangeable speed**, the timescale is much more reasonable than “real time,” but much slower than the maximum-speed example I gave earlier.

Your current default slider value is **1 interaction tick per real second**.

However, one interaction tick is not merely one ecological minute under the default observation setting. The standard observation mode is designed so that **one ecological year of 120 days takes 180 real minutes**.

So at default speed:

- **1 ecological day ≈ 1.5 real minutes**
    
- **10 ecological days ≈ 15 real minutes**
    
- **20 ecological days ≈ 30 real minutes**
    
- **60 ecological days ≈ 1.5 real hours**
    
- **120 ecological days = 1 ecological year ≈ 3 real hours**
    

That is the right timescale to use.

## What that means for animal generations

Some species reproduce much faster than others.

For example, the Meadow Nibbler matures at about 20 days and has an 18-day gestation. In an idealised case, a naturally born animal could therefore reach maturity after about **30 real minutes**, and a subsequent pregnancy could complete roughly **27 minutes** later. So its theoretical minimum birth-to-next-generation interval is around **an hour**, although actual mate finding, condition, courtship and conception will usually make it longer.

The Valley Grazer matures at 80 days and has a 60-day gestation. That corresponds to roughly:

- maturity: **2 real hours**
    
- gestation: **1.5 real hours**
    

So a newborn Valley Grazer producing offspring of its own would ordinarily require at least roughly **3.5 hours**, even before delays from ecology and mating.

A Ridge Hunter matures at 95 days with a 90-day gestation:

- maturity: about **2 hours 23 minutes**
    
- gestation: about **2 hours 15 minutes**
    
- minimum combined: roughly **4 hours 38 minutes**
    

Larger slow-life-history animals take longer still. A Great Plains Grazer matures at 130 days and gestates for 110 days, so a birth-to-next-generation cycle is approximately **6 real hours minimum**.

## So when would the game start to feel familiar?

Because dozens or hundreds of animals are acting **simultaneously**, you do not have to wait for one animal to demonstrate all 62 behaviours.

In the first **30 minutes**, the ecosystem has already experienced about 20 simulated days. With a healthy population, you would probably see a large amount of ordinary behaviour: movement, feeding, water seeking, rest, social contact, threat responses, group behaviour, hunting attempts, communication and environmental reactions.

After about **1–3 hours**, the world has experienced roughly 40–120 simulated days. That is enough for seasons and environmental conditions to change substantially and for reproductive processes among founding adults to produce naturally born offspring. Fast-lived species may already have descendants approaching maturity.

After about **5–10 hours**, you have passed through approximately **1.7–3.3 ecological years**. By then, I would expect most of the major behavioural systems to have appeared somewhere in a sufficiently diverse world: reproduction, births, nursing, caregiving, predation, carcass use, social conflict, dominance, submission, mate rejection, group formation, injuries, recovery, seasonal behaviour, deaths and lineage development.

After around **20–40 hours**, you are no longer mainly discovering basic mechanics. You are increasingly seeing **different manifestations of mechanics you already know**: unusual personalities, rare combinations of inherited traits, exceptional families, odd mate choices, unusual social conflicts, particular ecological crises, specialised animals, and multigenerational consequences.

## “Most things” is probably tens of hours

If the player could never alter the speed and simply observed normal worlds, I would estimate:

|Real play time|Approx. ecological time|Likely experience|
|---|--:|---|
|**1 hour**|40 days|Core ecosystem very visible|
|**3 hours**|1 year|Most common systems familiar|
|**6 hours**|2 years|First substantial generational stories|
|**12 hours**|4 years|Most major mechanics likely encountered|
|**24 hours**|8 years|Many rare and multigenerational combinations|
|**50 hours**|~16.7 years|Deep familiarity; new emergent stories still likely|
|**100 hours**|~33.3 years|Very broad coverage, but still not “everything”|

Those are estimates rather than guarantees because species composition, mortality, population size and world seed matter.

My rough answer for **“How long until I've seen most of what the game does?”** would therefore be:

# **about 10–30 hours at the fixed default speed**

assuming a reasonably populated, species-diverse world and that “most” means most **major systems and categories of behaviour**.

For someone deliberately exploring several different ecological setups, perhaps:

# **30–50 hours**

would give a very broad impression of the game's behavioural repertoire.

## But seeing every possible animal would be impossible

The 14-million / 890-million figures from before should not be interpreted as “there are 890 million scenes the player has to watch.”

Most of those are subtly different organisms and contexts.

For example, you could see `courtship` hundreds of times. But each case might involve different:

- female preferences,
    
- male traits,
    
- previous relationships,
    
- parental ancestry,
    
- body sizes,
    
- aggression,
    
- memory,
    
- reproductive history,
    
- resource conditions,
    
- fear,
    
- physiological condition.
    

So after perhaps 20 hours you may think:

> “I've seen courtship.”

But after 200 hours you could still encounter:

> a highly divergent, unusually persistent-memory female descended from a particular lineage rejecting a dominant male she previously observed during scarcity while preferring a smaller but better-foraging male.

That exact causal episode may never have happened before.

That is why “everything” is effectively unreachable.

## Evolution makes long play especially valuable

At default speed, evolution becomes something a human player can genuinely observe over an ordinary game session.

For a fast-lived species, you might get several generations in a single evening.

For slower species, generational turnover takes several hours.

After:

- **3 hours:** 1 ecological year
    
- **15 hours:** 5 years
    
- **30 hours:** 10 years
    
- **60 hours:** 20 years
    
- **150 hours:** 50 years
    

A 100-hour player could therefore have watched more than **33 ecological years** of uninterrupted history.

For a fast species maturing in 20 days, that could theoretically encompass many dozens of generations.

For slow species, perhaps several to a couple of dozen meaningful generations depending on survival and reproductive delay.

That means a long-term save could become radically different from its starting world.

The founding animals die.

Their children reproduce.

Those offspring inherit different combinations.

Selection and drift modify the population.

Relationships and group structures arise.

Some lineages disappear.

Others proliferate.

The player might know all the mechanics by hour 20 while still having **no idea what that particular world will look like at hour 100**.

That is probably the most meaningful answer: **at fixed normal speed, the game could be mechanically familiar within roughly 10–30 hours, deeply explored by perhaps 50–100 hours, yet remain emergently non-exhaustible because the content after that is generated by the history of the world rather than by a finite list of encounters.**
