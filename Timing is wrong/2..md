The new code changes the essay substantially. The most important revision is that inheritance is no longer hypothetical: the simulation explicitly derives a newborn’s traits from its mother and father, introduces bounded variation, applies prenatal-development effects, writes those traits into the living organism, and then measures their consequences across generations. The code also supports a meaningful discussion of **individual cognitive and behavioural divergence**, although I would use “neurodivergence” carefully as a simulation-level analogy rather than as a claim that the animals instantiate human clinical categories.

# Selectivity, Inheritance, Individual Divergence, and Evolution Across Generations in the Simulation

## Introduction

The simulation can now be described much more strongly than was possible from its reproductive behaviour alone. It does not merely contain animals that survive, choose mates, reproduce, and produce offspring. It contains an explicit generational architecture in which individual variation can be transmitted from parents to offspring, modified by developmental conditions, expressed through physiology, cognition, social behaviour, resource acquisition, mating behaviour, and caregiving, and then subjected to differential survival and reproductive success.

This distinction is fundamental.

A system in which individuals merely differ is not necessarily evolutionary. A system in which individuals reproduce at different rates is selective, but it still does not necessarily produce cumulative Darwinian evolution. For that, at least some of the characteristics affecting survival and reproduction must persist across generations through inheritance.

The current simulation satisfies that additional requirement.

Naturally born offspring do not receive entirely independent random phenotypes. The simulation identifies the mother and father, calculates parental averages for multiple traits, generates bounded offspring variation around those parental values, modifies the degree of developmental divergence according to prenatal condition, applies the resulting trait architecture directly to the newborn, records its lineage, and subsequently permits that offspring to survive, compete, learn, choose, reproduce, and become a parent itself. The birth path therefore connects one generation causally to the next.

The result is not a literal molecular-genetics simulation. There are no explicit chromosomes, alleles, recombination maps, dominance relations, nucleotide sequences, or Mendelian loci. Instead, the simulation implements a **quantitative inheritance model**. Traits are represented as continuous values. Offspring tend to resemble the parental midpoint while also exhibiting bounded stochastic variation. This is conceptually similar to representing complex polygenic phenotypes without representing the underlying genes individually.

This is sufficient for a large class of evolutionary phenomena. Natural selection does not require the simulation to know the molecular identity of a gene. It requires heritable variation that has consequences for survival and reproduction. The simulation now clearly provides such variation.

---

## 1. From Individual Difference to Evolutionary Variation

Every organism in the simulation is an individual rather than an interchangeable instance of a species template.

Species membership establishes broad biological constraints, but individual animals vary within those constraints. Among the characteristics visible in the current system are body size, aggression, sensory ability, water-acquisition ability, food-acquisition ability, mating ability, caregiving tendency, memory persistence, physical potential, social tendencies, personal-space preferences, reproductive history, physiological condition, remembered experience, and learned behaviour.

The Laboratory itself exposes many of these differences when an organism is inspected. An individual can be described in terms of its water ability, foraging or hunting ability, vigilance or scent ability, care affinity, aggression, memory persistence, personal-space preference, intrusion tolerance, mating history, and inherited trait profile.

This produces a population in which two animals of the same species can nevertheless confront the same situation differently.

One may remember a resource location for longer.

Another may be more aggressive.

Another may be better at locating water.

Another may be better at acquiring food.

Another may display greater caregiving behaviour.

Another may be unusually large.

Another may combine relatively ordinary overall capability with a highly unusual distribution of strengths and weaknesses.

The last case becomes particularly important when considering what might reasonably be called **neurodivergence within the simulation**.

---

# 2. What “Neurodivergence” Can Mean in This Simulation

The term _neurodivergence_ originates in human social and clinical contexts, and it would be scientifically misleading to state that a simulated animal is “autistic,” “ADHD,” or belongs to some other human diagnostic category merely because its computational parameters differ.

The simulation does not contain diagnostic models of autism, ADHD, dyslexia, Tourette syndrome, schizophrenia, or other human neurodevelopmental or psychiatric conditions.

It does, however, contain something more general that is highly relevant to the underlying concept of neurodiversity: **organisms can possess substantially different inherited cognitive and behavioural architectures without those architectures being arranged on a simple better–worse scale**.

This interpretation is directly supported by the simulation's inherited-trait representation.

Naturally born animals receive an inherited trait profile with two conceptually separate dimensions:

1. **overall capability**, and
    
2. **trait divergence**.
    

The Laboratory explicitly presents these as independent distributions. The first ranges approximately from broadly disadvantaged through typical to broadly exceptional. The second ranges from balanced through specialised to highly divergent. Crucially, the interface states that divergence redistributes strengths and weaknesses and **“is not a quality ranking.”**

That distinction is extremely important.

An organism can therefore be:

- broadly typical and balanced;
    
- broadly typical but highly divergent;
    
- broadly exceptional and balanced;
    
- broadly exceptional but specialised;
    
- broadly disadvantaged yet specialised in particular capacities;
    
- or many intermediate combinations.
    

The simulation is not saying:

> more divergent = worse.

Nor is it saying:

> more divergent = better.

Instead, divergence describes **unevenness across a multidimensional phenotype**.

That is much closer to a neurodiversity-style interpretation than a single intelligence score would be.

---

# 3. The Two Axes: General Capability and Divergence

The inheritance system generates an `overallScore` and a separate `divergenceScore`.

These variables do different things.

The **overall score** produces a broad shift affecting multiple offspring characteristics in the same general direction. An offspring at the positive end may receive somewhat elevated values across several characteristics; an offspring at the negative end may receive somewhat reduced values.

The **divergence score**, by contrast, controls how unevenly the offspring's individual characteristics are redistributed.

A low-divergence organism tends toward a relatively balanced trait profile.

A moderately divergent organism becomes more specialised.

A highly divergent organism may have substantial strengths in some dimensions and substantial weaknesses in others.

The simulation therefore represents at least two different forms of biological individuality.

One is:

**general level difference**

The other is:

**configuration difference**

These are not equivalent.

Consider two hypothetical animals whose overall inherited capability is approximately average.

Animal A might be highly balanced:

- average memory,
    
- average food skill,
    
- average water skill,
    
- average sensory ability,
    
- average aggression,
    
- average caregiving,
    
- average mating ability.
    

Animal B might have almost the same average overall level but be highly divergent:

- exceptional memory persistence,
    
- poor water acquisition,
    
- strong food acquisition,
    
- low aggression,
    
- high caregiving,
    
- weak scent ability,
    
- unusual mating competence.
    

Their average may be similar, but their lives could be radically different.

The simulation explicitly supports this kind of configuration because the offspring's individual trait deviations are distributed around their own mean and then multiplied by the divergence parameter.

This means that **difference itself becomes structured**.

---

# 4. Why This Matters for Neurodivergence

This architecture allows a useful distinction between **general impairment** and **specialisation**.

A highly divergent animal may fail more often in one class of problem and outperform other animals in another.

For example, an individual with unusually persistent memory might retain resource information longer but still possess poor immediate resource-acquisition ability.

Another animal might have high care affinity and low aggression, producing a strongly social or caregiving phenotype but reducing success in direct competitive encounters.

A third may possess high aggression and strong size but relatively weak memory persistence.

None of these configurations can be evaluated independently of context.

This is precisely where a neurodiversity-like interpretation becomes scientifically interesting.

The fitness of a cognitive or behavioural architecture depends upon the environment in which that architecture operates.

A highly persistent memory may be valuable in an environment where water sources are spatially stable.

The same persistence could potentially become less useful when remembered resource locations frequently become obsolete.

High caution may be valuable under intense predation.

The same caution may reduce opportunities in a safer environment.

High social susceptibility may facilitate coordination in stable groups but create disadvantages when group information is unreliable.

High independence may help an animal depart from a failing collective strategy but make social coordination more difficult.

The architecture therefore encourages thinking in terms of **ecological fit rather than universal cognitive superiority**.

---

# 5. Inherited Memory Persistence

Memory persistence is particularly important to the neurodivergence question because it is explicitly inherited.

The offspring inheritance system calculates a child's memory-persistence value from the corresponding parental values, with additional bounded variation.

The Laboratory then exposes memory persistence as an individual trait alongside aggression, resource skills, care tendencies, personal space, and other characteristics.

This gives the simulation a direct inherited cognitive dimension.

Parents with unusually persistent memory tend, on average, to produce offspring whose baseline is shifted toward their parental level, although offspring variation prevents exact cloning.

Over multiple generations, this allows selection to act on memory persistence.

If long memory consistently improves survival or reproduction, the population distribution could shift upward.

If excessive persistence becomes costly under a rapidly changing environment, intermediate or shorter persistence could instead be favoured.

If different ecological niches favour different memory strategies, stable polymorphism or divergent lineages could potentially emerge.

Thus cognition itself can become evolutionarily selectable.

---

# 6. Behavioural Architecture Extends Beyond Inherited Ecological Traits

The simulation also contains substantial individual variation in decision style.

The commitment system describes organisms using dimensions including:

- decisiveness;
    
- perseverance;
    
- commitment stability;
    
- flexibility;
    
- evidence threshold;
    
- social susceptibility;
    
- confidence.
    

The live Laboratory displays these parameters as an organism's **behavioural architecture**.

From these dimensions, broader behavioural styles can emerge, including descriptions such as:

- steadfast;
    
- adaptive;
    
- socially cautious;
    
- assertive;
    
- deliberative;
    
- measured.
    

These are not merely cosmetic labels. They arise from underlying differences in how an organism maintains plans, responds to evidence, changes decisions, tolerates social pressure, and persists in current commitments.

This adds a second level of cognitive diversity.

The inherited trait architecture determines certain biological and behavioural predispositions, while lifetime decision processes express those predispositions under current physiological and environmental conditions.

Consequently:

**genotype-like inherited parameters are not identical to behaviour.**

Behaviour emerges from inherited predisposition interacting with:

- current energy;
    
- hydration;
    
- fatigue;
    
- fear;
    
- social support;
    
- perceived danger;
    
- memory;
    
- learned protocols;
    
- environmental constraints;
    
- current goals.
    

This is closer to biological reality than simply treating personality as a fixed number.

---

# 7. Neurodivergence as a Dynamic Phenotype

The most defensible interpretation is therefore not that each animal has a fixed diagnostic “neurotype,” but that each animal possesses an **individual cognitive phenotype**.

That phenotype has several sources:

**Inherited disposition**

Traits such as memory persistence, aggression, care affinity, resource skills, body size, and mating skill partly descend from parents.

**Developmental variation**

Prenatal condition affects the divergence architecture of the offspring.

**Individual stochastic variation**

Offspring are not exact averages of their parents.

**Learning**

Animals develop memories and modify behaviour according to experience.

**Physiological state**

Hunger, dehydration, exhaustion, injury, stress, fear, and other conditions modify expressed behaviour.

**Social history**

Relationships, mating outcomes, caregiving, conflict, group membership, and social information affect later choices.

**Environmental context**

Terrain, resources, weather, predation, population density, and ecological uncertainty determine which characteristics are useful.

Consequently, the same inherited architecture does not guarantee the same lifetime behaviour.

This creates an important distinction between **trait architecture** and **expressed organisation**.

---

# 8. Inheritance Is Explicit, Not Hypothetical

The earlier version of this analysis had to remain cautious about inheritance because the actual newborn-construction path had not been located.

That uncertainty can now be removed.

When an animal gives birth, the simulation identifies the stored father of the pregnancy, creates the newborn, evaluates prenatal condition, constructs an offspring trait architecture using the mother and father, applies that architecture to the newborn, rescales the newborn's physical body to reflect its inherited size, registers kinship, records parent–offspring relationships, and stores lineage information.

This is direct parent-to-offspring inheritance.

The child therefore does not merely happen to resemble its species.

Its individual values are causally related to its parents.

---

# 9. Quantitative Parental Inheritance

For the directly parent-derived characteristics, the inheritance process begins by calculating the average of the available parental values.

Conceptually:

**mother's trait + father's trait → parental midpoint**

The offspring is then generated around that midpoint.

This is not Mendelian inheritance in the strict genetic sense. There are no discrete alleles such as `A` and `a`.

Instead it resembles a simplified **quantitative genetic model**, appropriate for complex traits that in real organisms might depend on many genes and developmental influences.

For body size, for example, an unusually large mother and unusually large father tend to produce offspring with a large baseline body-size tendency.

Two smaller parents tend to shift the offspring baseline downward.

But the offspring is not exactly equal to either parent or to their precise arithmetic midpoint.

Variation is introduced around the parental value.

That variation is what permits novel combinations to appear.

---

# 10. The Inherited Trait Set

The current inheritance architecture includes at least eight characteristics whose baselines are explicitly derived from parental values:

- body-size tendency;
    
- aggression;
    
- scent or vigilance ability;
    
- water-acquisition ability;
    
- food-acquisition ability;
    
- mating ability;
    
- caregiving affinity;
    
- memory persistence.
    

It also generates two broader biological potentials:

- health potential;
    
- strength potential.
    

This is far more extensive than the original body-size interpretation.

The simulation therefore contains inherited variation affecting several functional domains simultaneously.

### Morphology

Body size.

### Temperament

Aggression and caregiving tendency.

### Perception

Scent or vigilance ability.

### Ecology

Water and food acquisition.

### Reproduction

Mating ability.

### Cognition

Memory persistence.

### Physiological capacity

Health and strength potential.

Evolution in the simulation can therefore act upon combinations rather than isolated traits.

---

# 11. Correlated Evolution and Trait Combinations

This is important because selection rarely acts on isolated characteristics.

Suppose a lineage becomes larger and more aggressive.

This may improve dominance and reproductive competition.

But if those animals also inherit poor food-acquisition ability, their increased energetic needs could produce a strong survival cost.

Alternatively, a lineage might develop:

- high memory persistence;
    
- high water skill;
    
- moderate body size;
    
- low aggression;
    
- high caregiving.
    

Such animals might perform particularly well in a stable social herbivore environment.

Another ecological context might favour almost the opposite combination.

The simulation's divergence architecture therefore permits **trait syndromes** to emerge.

A lineage does not necessarily evolve one universally superior trait.

It can evolve configurations.

That is arguably more interesting than simple directional evolution.

---

# 12. Offspring Variation Is Structured Rather Than Independent Noise

The stochastic component is also more sophisticated than simply assigning every trait an unrelated random mutation.

The simulation first generates an overall offspring-level variation.

It then generates a divergence magnitude.

It creates multiple deviations whose average is normalized.

These deviations redistribute the offspring's abilities relative to one another.

The result is that an offspring can be broadly elevated or reduced while simultaneously being unusually specialised in particular dimensions.

This produces variation with internal structure.

An offspring may therefore inherit approximately ordinary parental characteristics but develop an unusual combination.

That is precisely the type of architecture needed if the simulation is to produce lineages that are not merely “better” or “worse” but **differently organised**.

---

# 13. Prenatal Condition Influences Developmental Divergence

Another important feature is that inheritance is not isolated from development.

The pregnancy system calculates prenatal quality before birth. The offspring inheritance system receives this value and uses poorer prenatal quality to increase the potential degree of trait divergence.

This means the simulation distinguishes:

**what comes from the parents**

from:

**what happens during development.**

That is conceptually important.

The offspring phenotype is therefore not represented as:

**genes → fixed organism**

but more approximately as:

**parental inheritance + developmental condition + stochastic variation → offspring phenotype**

This creates a primitive model of developmental plasticity.

---

# 14. Prenatal Divergence Is Not Genetic Mutation

A methodological distinction is necessary here.

If difficult pregnancy conditions increase the offspring's divergence, that particular developmental effect should not automatically be interpreted as a genetic mutation.

The resulting offspring phenotype can be unusual because of developmental conditions even when the parental inheritance baseline is ordinary.

Whether that developmental difference itself becomes heritable depends upon whether it changes one of the stored parental traits that later contributes to reproduction.

Therefore the model contains both:

**heritable parent-derived variation**

and:

**developmentally generated phenotypic variation.**

These should remain analytically distinct.

---

# 15. The Child's Inherited Size Actually Changes Its Body

Body-size inheritance is especially well integrated.

A newborn is initially produced through the general animal constructor. The birth process then remembers that temporary generated size, replaces the relevant phenotype with the inherited architecture, and rescales body mass, lean mass, and fat mass according to the inherited `sizeTrait`.

This means the inherited value is not merely stored as metadata.

It changes the physical organism.

That physical change can then influence later ecological and social outcomes.

Inheritance therefore participates in the causal mechanics of the world.

---

# 16. Heritable Body Size and Sexual Selection

Body size provides one of the clearest complete evolutionary chains.

Body size is inherited.

Body size contributes to social temperament and dominance.

Dominance can influence social competition.

Social competition can affect reproductive access.

Female mate preferences can include preferred mass.

Consequently, large or small inherited body forms can experience differential reproductive success.

The pathway is:

**parental size → offspring size → adult phenotype → social/reproductive consequences → reproductive success → descendant size**

This is genuine sexual-selection architecture.

---

# 17. Female Mate Choice Is Individual Rather Than Random

Females do not simply mate with the nearest eligible male.

Adult females possess individual mate preferences.

These preferences include dimensions such as:

- preferred body mass;
    
- preferred age;
    
- preferred aggression;
    
- observed libido;
    
- preferred mating duration;
    
- injury tolerance;
    
- foraging behaviour;
    
- caregiving behaviour;
    
- calm movement;
    
- attentiveness.
    

The Laboratory exposes these preferences as part of the inspected animal's relationship and reproductive profile.

This makes mate selection a multidimensional process.

A male can fit one female well and another poorly.

Consequently, reproductive value is relational rather than absolute.

---

# 18. Sexual Selection Can Act on Behavioural and Cognitive Traits

This becomes particularly important now that mating skill, care affinity, aggression, memory persistence, and ecological skills can differ among lineages.

Sexual selection need not act exclusively on obvious physical traits.

Suppose females increasingly favour reliable foragers under resource scarcity.

If food-acquisition ability has an inherited component, mate preference can indirectly select for that trait.

Suppose females increasingly value caregiving behaviour in an environment where offspring survival strongly depends on parental protection.

Because care affinity is inherited, selection could favour higher caregiving tendencies.

Suppose social stability rewards individuals that remember partners and resource locations effectively.

Inherited memory persistence could become indirectly involved in reproductive success.

Sexual selection can therefore extend into the simulation's cognitive and behavioural architecture.

---

# 19. Female Preferences Are Themselves Adaptive During Life

Female preference is not merely a static inherited rule.

Females can modify mate preferences according to observed males and environmental pressure.

This introduces **behavioural plasticity** into sexual selection.

The female does not genetically change because the environment becomes dangerous.

Instead:

**environment → experience → preference change → altered mating decisions**

If those altered mating decisions repeatedly favour certain heritable male characteristics, then individual learning can change the evolutionary pressure experienced by the population.

This creates an important bridge between two timescales.

### Within a lifetime

The organism learns.

### Across generations

Learning changes which inherited variants reproduce.

This is a powerful feature of the simulation.

---

# 20. Learning Can Modify Selection Without Being Inherited

This distinction deserves emphasis.

Suppose a female learns to prefer highly attentive males after repeated predator encounters.

The learned preference itself does not need to be genetically transmitted for evolution to occur.

It modifies who reproduces.

If attentiveness correlates with heritable traits, descendants carrying those traits may become more common.

Thus:

**non-heritable learning can alter the selection environment acting on heritable variation.**

That is biologically plausible and conceptually important.

---

# 21. Male Reproductive Strategies Also Differ

Males are not reproductively identical.

Their reproductive behaviour depends upon physiological condition, aggression, libido, social relationships, courtship breadth, caregiving tendencies, and previous mating history.

Some may adopt broad courtship behaviour.

Some may become selective.

Some may develop relatively stable partner bonds.

Therefore reproductive success emerges from an interaction between:

- male phenotype;
    
- male behavioural strategy;
    
- female preference;
    
- previous social history;
    
- physiological condition;
    
- ecological opportunity.
    

This substantially increases the number of routes through which sexual selection can occur.

---

# 22. Reproduction Is a Sequence of Selective Filters

The reproductive architecture also separates the stages of reproduction.

Courtship is not conception.

Acceptance is not birth.

The process contains distinct stages:

**mate encounter → courtship → evaluation → acceptance or rejection → mating → conception → pregnancy → embryonic survival → labour → birth → dependent survival**

The pregnancy code even permits conception to fail, embryonic loss to occur, miscarriage to occur, and prenatal condition to affect offspring.

Each of these stages can alter realized reproductive success.

An organism can therefore:

- survive to adulthood but never mate;
    
- mate but fail to conceive;
    
- conceive but lose the pregnancy;
    
- produce offspring in poor prenatal condition;
    
- produce healthy offspring that subsequently die;
    
- or produce descendants that themselves survive and reproduce.
    

This is a much richer representation of fitness than simply counting mating events.

---

# 23. Fitness Means Descendant Contribution

The simulation's generational audit reinforces this interpretation.

It does not define reproductive success merely as “mated successfully.”

It counts surviving offspring associated with each parent and compares trait profiles against reproductive outcomes.

This is much closer to the evolutionary meaning of fitness.

An individual can therefore be:

- physically dominant;
    
- long-lived;
    
- socially influential;
    
- highly intelligent in some behavioural sense;
    

and nevertheless possess low evolutionary fitness if it leaves few descendants.

Conversely, a physically unremarkable animal may contribute disproportionately to future generations.

---

# 24. Natural Selection Acts Simultaneously

Sexual selection is only one component.

The same inherited traits operate in an ecological world containing:

- limited food;
    
- limited water;
    
- weather;
    
- terrain;
    
- predators;
    
- competitors;
    
- injuries;
    
- fatigue;
    
- energetic costs;
    
- pregnancy costs;
    
- caregiving demands;
    
- resource uncertainty.
    

Natural selection can therefore favour traits that improve survival independently of mating preference.

Water skill is a straightforward example.

If animals differ genetically in their ability to acquire water and drought creates intense hydration pressure, animals with higher water skill may survive disproportionately.

If surviving individuals reproduce, water skill can increase across generations.

The same reasoning applies to food skill, sensory ability, body size, aggression, memory persistence, health potential, and strength potential.

---

# 25. Natural and Sexual Selection Can Conflict

One of the most interesting possibilities is that natural and sexual selection may favour different trait values.

Suppose females favour large males.

Large males obtain more mating opportunities.

But large bodies require more energy.

During abundant seasons:

**sexual advantage > energetic cost**

Large size may spread.

During famine:

**energetic cost > mating advantage**

Smaller animals may survive more effectively.

The population trajectory then depends on the combined outcome of:

- mating advantage;
    
- survival probability;
    
- reproduction frequency;
    
- offspring survival.
    

There is no need for the simulation to declare which trait is optimal.

The outcome emerges from the ecology.

---

# 26. Neurodivergent-Like Traits Can Also Experience Trade-Offs

The same logic applies to cognitive and behavioural variation.

Consider memory persistence.

Long memory may help animals retain water locations or social relationships.

But an environment that changes quickly could make old information misleading.

Consider high flexibility.

Flexible organisms may abandon failed plans quickly.

But excessive switching could prevent completion of difficult long-term objectives.

Consider perseverance.

Perseverance may help an animal maintain a difficult but correct plan.

The same persistence may become dangerous when conditions change.

Consider social susceptibility.

High susceptibility may support coordinated group behaviour.

It may also cause an animal to follow an incorrect group strategy.

Low susceptibility may promote independent correction.

It may also reduce coordination.

These are not simply good and bad traits.

They are context-sensitive strategies.

This is where the simulation's behavioural diversity becomes particularly compatible with a neurodiversity interpretation.

---

# 27. Divergence Is Not Disability by Definition

The simulation's own language explicitly avoids equating divergence with inferiority.

A highly divergent profile describes the distribution of traits, not overall quality.

This produces four conceptually different possibilities:

### Low overall capability, low divergence

Broad limitations across many dimensions.

### High overall capability, low divergence

Broad strengths across many dimensions.

### Typical overall capability, high divergence

Strong peaks and troughs despite an ordinary overall mean.

### High overall capability, high divergence

Exceptional general ability combined with pronounced specialisation.

This is much richer than representing organisms on a single “fitness” axis.

---

# 28. Neurodivergence as Organisational Difference

At a theoretical level, the simulation suggests another interpretation.

An organism's cognitive phenotype is not represented by one variable.

It arises from an **organisation of interacting variables**.

Memory affects what evidence remains available.

Evidence thresholds affect when information is sufficient.

Flexibility affects whether a commitment is revised.

Perseverance affects whether it continues.

Social susceptibility affects how other organisms alter its behaviour.

Fear and physiological strain temporarily modify these tendencies.

Experience creates learned protocols.

Current needs change priorities.

Consequently, two animals can process effectively the same world through different internal organisations.

The important variable is not merely how much cognition each animal possesses.

It is **how cognition is organised**.

This is arguably the strongest sense in which the model contains a form of simulated neurodiversity.

---

# 29. Development Produces Individual Minds, Not Static Trait Bundles

Inherited differences do not completely determine later behaviour.

An inherited trait architecture establishes tendencies.

The organism then accumulates:

- perceptual encounters;
    
- memories;
    
- successful and failed resource searches;
    
- social relationships;
    
- mating history;
    
- injury history;
    
- learned protocols;
    
- fear responses;
    
- predictive outcomes;
    
- caregiving relationships;
    
- group experience.
    

Two siblings with similar inherited values can therefore diverge substantially during life.

This gives the simulation three important levels of individuality:

### Genetic-like individuality

Different inherited quantitative traits.

### Developmental individuality

Different prenatal condition and stochastic developmental divergence.

### Experiential individuality

Different histories of perception, learning, relationships, injury, ecology, and action.

The mature phenotype is the product of all three.

---

# 30. Selection Acts on the Expressed Organism, Not the Hidden Trait Alone

Evolution does not directly “see” an abstract `memoryPersistence` number.

Selection acts on consequences.

If higher memory persistence improves water acquisition, survival increases.

If high aggression improves mating success but increases injury, both effects matter.

If high care affinity increases offspring survival, the trait may spread even if it reduces the parent's opportunities elsewhere.

If extreme specialisation provides advantages only in a narrow ecological niche, its fitness depends on whether that niche persists.

This is a crucial point.

Selection operates through the entire organism–environment relationship.

---

# 31. Individual Differences Can Become Lineage Differences

Because parent values influence offspring values, repeated reproduction can create lineage continuity.

Suppose one family happens to possess:

- high memory persistence;
    
- above-average water ability;
    
- moderate aggression;
    
- strong care affinity.
    

If this combination produces above-average descendant survival, that lineage may gradually expand.

Another lineage may possess:

- large body size;
    
- high aggression;
    
- high mating skill;
    
- lower caregiving.
    

It may dominate reproduction under one ecological regime but collapse during prolonged scarcity.

A third lineage may be highly divergent, with exceptional food acquisition but poor water acquisition.

Its success may depend strongly on local hydrology.

The simulation can therefore produce lineage-specific evolutionary histories.

---

# 32. Recombination-Like Averaging Prevents Exact Cloning

Offspring do not simply copy the mother or father.

The parental midpoint combines both.

This matters because sexual reproduction can generate intermediate phenotypes and novel combinations.

An aggressive father and calm mother can produce an intermediate baseline with additional variation.

A high-memory parent and moderate-memory parent produce a shifted midpoint.

Multiple parental characteristics are recombined statistically within the same offspring.

The model therefore represents a simplified analogue of sexual recombination even without explicit chromosomes.

---

# 33. Variation Allows Selection to Continue

If offspring were exact copies of the parental midpoint every time, population variation would gradually collapse.

The stochastic variation system prevents this.

Each generation continues to produce novelty around parental baselines.

That novelty provides new material for selection.

The process therefore contains all three classical Darwinian ingredients:

**variation**

Individuals differ.

**heritability**

Offspring resemble their parents.

**differential reproductive success**

Some variants contribute more descendants than others.

This is sufficient for Darwinian evolution.

---

# 34. The System Also Models Drift

The presence of inheritance does not mean every generational change is adaptive.

The generational audit explicitly warns about:

- small population size;
    
- demographic drift;
    
- founder effects;
    
- reproductive skew;
    
- inadequate naturally born sample sizes.
    

This is scientifically important because an inherited trait can become common purely because its carriers happened to reproduce more in a small population.

The model therefore recognizes the distinction:

**selection ≠ every change in trait frequency**

Some change may be stochastic.

---

# 35. Neurodivergence Can Also Drift

This applies directly to cognitive diversity.

Suppose a highly divergent individual happens to produce many descendants because of historical circumstance rather than because divergence itself was advantageous.

Its descendants may inherit unusual trait distributions.

The population could therefore become cognitively more divergent through drift.

Conversely, a rare specialised cognitive profile might disappear without ever being strongly selected against.

This means that future analysis should distinguish:

- selection for divergence;
    
- selection against divergence;
    
- indirect selection on correlated traits;
    
- neutral persistence;
    
- founder effects;
    
- stochastic loss.
    

---

# 36. Selection on Divergence Itself Is Not Yet Guaranteed

An important methodological qualification remains.

The simulation generates a divergence score, but natural selection does not necessarily act directly on the numerical `divergenceScore`.

What selection acts upon are the actual component traits produced by that divergence.

A highly divergent animal may succeed because one of its specialised capacities is useful.

Another highly divergent animal may fail because one of its weaknesses is costly.

Therefore one should not automatically conclude:

> evolution favours neurodivergence.

or:

> evolution suppresses neurodivergence.

Instead, the appropriate question is:

> Under what ecological and social conditions do particular patterns of cognitive and behavioural divergence persist or increase?

That is a much more scientifically defensible research question.

---

# 37. Neurodiversity Can Be an Emergent Population Property

Once many generations exist, the population can itself be described in terms of diversity of cognitive architectures.

One could measure:

- mean memory persistence;
    
- variance in memory persistence;
    
- proportion of balanced profiles;
    
- proportion of specialised profiles;
    
- proportion of highly divergent profiles;
    
- variance in aggression;
    
- variance in care affinity;
    
- combinations of ecological skills;
    
- reproductive success by divergence category;
    
- survival by divergence category;
    
- offspring survival by parental profile.
    

The generational audit already measures distributions of overall capability and divergence among naturally born animals.

This provides the beginning of a genuine **population neurodiversity metric**.

---

# 38. Different Ecologies Could Produce Different Cognitive Populations

This offers several compelling experiments.

### Stable-resource environment

Long memory persistence may become strongly advantageous.

### Rapidly fluctuating resources

Flexibility and lower reliance on old information may become more valuable.

### High predation

Vigilance, caution, social coordination, and memory of threats may become important.

### Low predation

Bold exploration could become less costly.

### Highly social ecology

Care affinity and social susceptibility may increase reproductive success.

### Fragmented ecology

Independent strategies could become more advantageous.

### Severe seasonal variation

Specialised lineages may flourish in some seasons and fail in others.

The result could be different cognitive distributions emerging from the same initial population.

---

# 39. Sexual Selection Could Maintain Cognitive Diversity

Female preferences add another mechanism.

Different females possess different mate preferences and modify them according to experience.

Therefore the population does not necessarily converge toward one male phenotype.

Some females may prefer greater mass.

Others may place more value on calm behaviour.

Others may value care.

Others may respond strongly to foraging behaviour.

This variation in preferences can maintain several viable reproductive strategies simultaneously.

In evolutionary terms, heterogeneous mate choice can help preserve polymorphism.

In neurodiversity terms, heterogeneous social preference can prevent one behavioural architecture from becoming universally dominant.

---

# 40. Social Behaviour Can Become Part of the Evolutionary Environment

Each animal is not merely adapting to terrain and resources.

Other animals constitute part of its environment.

A highly aggressive phenotype changes the pressures experienced by neighbours.

A highly caregiving phenotype changes dependent survival.

A dominant individual changes group relationships.

A persistent social learner can transmit behaviours.

A female's learned preference changes reproductive opportunities for males.

Consequently organisms modify the selective environment of one another.

This creates potential **frequency-dependent selection**.

A trait can be advantageous when rare and disadvantageous when common.

---

# 41. Caregiving Is Particularly Interesting

Care affinity is inherited and can affect behaviour toward offspring and group members.

This introduces an important indirect route to evolutionary fitness.

An animal does not improve fitness only by producing more offspring.

It may improve fitness by increasing the survival probability of offspring it already has.

High caregiving may therefore produce a trade-off:

**fewer reproductive opportunities**

but:

**higher offspring survival**

Whether selection favours high care depends on the balance.

This is particularly relevant in species with long dependency periods.

---

# 42. Memory and Social Inheritance Are Different Processes

The simulation also contains social learning and memory transmission mechanisms.

These must not be confused with genetic inheritance.

There are therefore at least two channels through which behavioural regularities can persist:

### Biological inheritance

Parent-derived trait values.

### Cultural or social transmission

Learned information, shared observations, behavioural protocols, or social experience.

This opens the possibility of gene–culture-like interaction at a simplified level.

A genetically persistent-memory lineage may learn more effectively.

A socially transmitted strategy may change which inherited traits are favoured.

These processes can interact without being identical.

---

# 43. Predictive Cognition Adds a Further Layer of Individuality

Animals also possess bounded predictive systems.

Their predictions depend on their own physiology, perception, memory, communicated evidence, and internal models.

Because inherited memory persistence, sensory abilities, ecological competence, social history, and learned experience differ, animals need not generate identical predictive situations even when occupying similar environments.

This adds another emergent cognitive dimension.

Different organisms can possess:

**different evidence**

leading to:

**different forecasts**

leading to:

**different decisions**

leading to:

**different consequences**

leading to:

**different learning histories.**

Small inherited differences can therefore become amplified through developmental feedback.

---

# 44. Cognitive Divergence Can Be Self-Reinforcing

Consider two siblings.

One inherits slightly higher memory persistence.

It remembers a water source longer.

Because it successfully revisits that water source, it survives drought with less physiological stress.

That successful experience strengthens its behavioural history.

Its sibling forgets the source sooner, explores elsewhere, experiences greater dehydration, and learns a different route.

Their initial inherited difference was small.

Their lifetime histories become increasingly different.

This is an example of developmental amplification.

The simulation's architecture permits this kind of divergence.

---

# 45. Evolution Is Therefore Multilevel in Time

The simulation now contains several distinct temporal processes.

### Immediate regulation

Energy, hydration, fear, fatigue, and bodily state change moment by moment.

### Behavioural adaptation

Animals choose actions in response to current conditions.

### Learning

Memory and successful or unsuccessful strategies alter future decisions.

### Social development

Relationships, mate histories, caregiving, alliances, and group structures accumulate.

### Reproduction

Some animals contribute offspring and others do not.

### Inheritance

Parental characteristics influence offspring traits.

### Evolution

Trait distributions can change across generations.

The important point is that none of these processes should be collapsed into one another.

---

# 46. Within-Lifetime Corrigibility and Across-Generation Selection

This provides a particularly interesting conceptual distinction.

An individual animal can correct itself during life.

It can:

- abandon a failing route;
    
- revise a plan;
    
- learn a resource location;
    
- change mate preference;
    
- respond to danger;
    
- adjust social behaviour.
    

That is within-lifetime adaptation.

Evolution operates differently.

Across generations:

- some architectures survive;
    
- some reproduce;
    
- some produce more surviving descendants;
    
- offspring inherit modified versions of parental architectures.
    

The two systems interact.

A highly corrigible organism may survive better and therefore reproduce more.

If some predispositions supporting that corrigibility are heritable, selection can gradually favour them.

Thus individual adaptation can itself become an object of evolutionary selection.

---

# 47. The Simulation Does Not Contain a Predetermined Evolutionary Goal

Nothing in this architecture requires evolution to move toward:

- intelligence;
    
- cooperation;
    
- aggression;
    
- larger bodies;
    
- smaller bodies;
    
- neurotypicality;
    
- neurodivergence;
    
- complexity;
    
- sociality.
    

Selection has no foresight.

A trait becomes common only insofar as historical ecological and reproductive processes cause its carriers to leave more descendants.

This is especially important for divergence.

A highly specialised phenotype is not a higher evolutionary stage.

It is one possible organisation.

Its future depends on ecological fit.

---

# 48. Evolution Can Reverse Direction

Suppose several generations favour large, aggressive males.

The population shifts toward that configuration.

Then a prolonged drought occurs.

Large bodies become expensive.

Aggressive encounters produce costly injuries.

Water scarcity favours efficient resource acquisition and memory.

The selection regime changes.

Previously successful lineages may decline.

Different lineages may expand.

Evolution therefore remains contingent.

The population has history but no destination.

---

# 49. Neurodivergent Populations Could Be Maintained by Environmental Variability

Environmental variability may actually preserve cognitive diversity.

If one strategy were always superior, the population might converge.

But when conditions alternate:

- persistent memory may help in stable years;
    
- flexibility may help in unstable years;
    
- aggression may help under competition;
    
- cooperation may help under predation;
    
- specialisation may help in predictable niches;
    
- generalism may help during ecological disruption.
    

Different architectures can remain viable.

This is one plausible mechanism through which simulated neurodiversity could persist.

---

# 50. The Simulation Can Now Test Evolutionary Questions

The most important consequence is methodological.

The simulation is no longer merely capable of illustrating evolution.

It can generate explicit evolutionary hypotheses.

For example:

**Does high memory persistence increase reproductive success during drought?**

**Does greater inherited body size increase mating success but reduce survival under food scarcity?**

**Does care affinity increase the number of offspring surviving to adulthood?**

**Do highly divergent animals outperform balanced animals in heterogeneous environments?**

**Do specialised cognitive profiles disappear in stable environments or persist through frequency-dependent selection?**

**Does female mate-choice learning amplify natural selection or oppose it?**

**Does social learning reduce genetic selection by allowing poorly adapted inherited phenotypes to compensate behaviourally?**

**Does behavioural corrigibility itself produce a reproductive advantage?**

These are now experimentally meaningful questions within the model.

# 52. Distinguishing Selection From Drift

Repeated seeds are essential.

Imagine that highly divergent organisms dominate one run.

That does not prove divergence was selected.

Perhaps one highly divergent founder happened to have many offspring.

Perhaps several competitors died randomly.

Perhaps the population was too small.

The simulation's existing drift warnings correctly recognise this problem.

Evidence of selection would require replicated runs showing a consistent relationship between phenotype and reproductive success under controlled conditions.

# 53. A More Accurate Description of the Current System

The original essay concluded cautiously that the simulation had strong selection mechanisms but that inheritance remained to be confirmed.

That statement is now obsolete.

The current evidence supports a substantially stronger description:

> The simulation implements a quantitative generational inheritance system in which naturally born animals receive multiple parent-derived traits, bounded offspring variation, developmental modification associated with prenatal condition, explicit kinship and lineage records, and individual cognitive, behavioural, ecological, physiological, and reproductive characteristics that can influence survival and reproductive success.

It therefore contains the principal ingredients necessary for Darwinian evolution.

---

# 54. What Is Still Simplified

This should nevertheless not be overstated.

The simulation does **not** currently constitute a molecular genetic model.

Its inheritance system does not explicitly represent:

- DNA;
    
- genes;
    
- chromosomes;
    
- alleles;
    
- dominant/recessive relationships;
    
- recombination loci;
    
- linkage disequilibrium;
    
- mutation at nucleotide level;
    
- epigenetic molecular mechanisms;
    
- meiosis;
    
- sexual chromosomes beyond simulated sex assignment;
    
- population-genetic allele frequencies.
    

The appropriate interpretation is therefore:

**quantitative inherited phenotype simulation**

rather than:

**genome simulation**.

For the behavioural and ecological questions being explored, this may actually be the more computationally appropriate abstraction.

---

# 55. What “Mutation” Means Here

The random offspring variation is mutation-like in its evolutionary role but should not automatically be described as literal genetic mutation.

It introduces novel heritable trait values around parental baselines.

Functionally, that supplies the variation on which selection can act.

Mechanistically, however, it is a stochastic quantitative inheritance operator.

A scientifically precise description would therefore use terms such as:

- offspring variation;
    
- quantitative inheritance;
    
- stochastic developmental variation;
    
- mutation-like variation;
    

rather than claiming literal DNA mutation.

---

# 56. What Neurodivergence Means Here

The same caution applies to neurodivergence.

A precise description would be:

> The simulation contains inherited and acquired variation in cognitive and behavioural architecture, including memory persistence, ecological competence, aggression, caregiving disposition, decision persistence, flexibility, evidence thresholds, social susceptibility, confidence, prediction, learning, and individual experience. Naturally born animals can also be classified as balanced, specialised, or highly divergent according to the unevenness of their inherited multidimensional trait profile.

This is a meaningful model of **neurocognitive diversity**.

It is not a clinical model of human neurodevelopmental conditions.

---

# 57. Why the Distinction Matters

Avoiding human diagnostic labels actually makes the evolutionary analysis stronger.

Instead of asking:

> Is this animal autistic?

one can ask:

> How uneven is this organism's inherited cognitive and behavioural architecture?

> Which abilities are unusually high?

> Which are unusually low?

> How does that configuration affect survival?

> Does it alter social relationships?

> Does it alter mating success?

> Does it alter learning?

> Does it alter reproductive contribution?

> Do descendants inherit related configurations?

These questions are measurable inside the simulation.

# 58. A Possible Operational Definition of Simulated Neurodivergence

For research purposes, one could operationally define a **divergent cognitive phenotype** without importing human diagnoses.

For example:

> A simulated organism is cognitively divergent when its inherited and expressed cognitive-behavioural characteristics are distributed substantially farther from the population's ordinary multidimensional configuration than expected from balanced individual variation.

The existing `divergenceScore` already supplies part of such a measure.

However, a future dedicated neurodiversity metric could focus specifically on cognitive dimensions rather than mixing morphological and behavioural traits.

Such dimensions might include:

- memory persistence;
    
- evidence threshold;
    
- flexibility;
    
- perseverance;
    
- social susceptibility;
    
- confidence;
    
- vigilance;
    
- learning rate;
    
- prediction calibration;
    
- exploratory tendency;
    
- sensory weighting;
    
- threat sensitivity.
    

That would separate general phenotypic divergence from specifically neurocognitive divergence.

# 59. Neurodivergence Could Become Evolutionarily Heritable

At least one explicitly cognitive characteristic, memory persistence, is already parent-derived.

Other behaviourally important traits such as aggression and care affinity are also inherited.

This means that the population can acquire lineage-level differences in cognitive-behavioural organisation.

If those configurations influence reproductive success, they can evolve.

Therefore the simulation already supports a limited form of:

**evolution of neurocognitive diversity.**

Again, this should not be confused with the evolution of specific human diagnoses.

It refers to the evolution of different information-processing, social, behavioural, and memory-related phenotypes.

# 60. Evolutionary Success Does Not Require Normality

One of the most interesting implications is that being near the population average does not necessarily maximize fitness.

A highly specialised animal may succeed exceptionally well in a particular ecological role.

A balanced generalist may survive environmental instability better.

A broadly exceptional animal may still fail through poor behavioural decisions.

A broadly disadvantaged animal may nevertheless reproduce successfully because one specialised trait strongly fits the local environment.

Evolution selects outcomes, not normality.

This makes the simulation capable of exploring why populations can maintain substantial individual variation rather than converging on a single ideal organism.

# Conclusion

The simulation clearly implements both **selection** and **inheritance**.

Individuals vary in morphology, temperament, ecological competence, cognition, social behaviour, reproductive strategy, and developmental history. Females select among males according to individual and adaptive preferences. Males differ in reproductive behaviour. Courtship, mating, conception, pregnancy, prenatal survival, birth, caregiving, and later offspring survival provide multiple reproductive filters.

Most importantly, offspring are genuinely connected to their parents.

The newborn inheritance system calculates several offspring traits from maternal and paternal values, introduces bounded quantitative variation, modifies developmental divergence according to prenatal condition, applies the resulting architecture directly to the newborn organism, adjusts physical phenotype accordingly, and preserves kinship and lineage.

The simulation therefore contains the essential Darwinian sequence:

**heritable variation + differential survival/reproduction + repeated generations = evolutionary change.**

The code also introduces a particularly interesting dimension that was absent from the earlier analysis: naturally born animals possess an inherited profile that distinguishes **overall capability** from **trait divergence**. Balanced, specialised, and highly divergent organisms can exist independently of whether their general capability is low, ordinary, or high. The simulation explicitly states that divergence redistributes strengths and weaknesses rather than ranking quality.

This provides a defensible foundation for discussing **simulated neurodiversity**.

Animals can differ in memory persistence, ecological skill, aggression, caregiving, evidence use, commitment, flexibility, perseverance, social susceptibility, confidence, predictive processing, learning history, and behavioural strategy. These differences interact with physiology and environment rather than functioning as fixed labels. Some are inherited, some develop through experience, and some are altered by current state.

Accordingly, the most scientifically useful question is no longer simply whether the simulation permits preferred traits to pass between generations.

It does.

The deeper question is now:

> **Which inherited organisations of physical, cognitive, behavioural, and social traits persist under which environments, and why?**

A related question is:

> **Does evolution favour general capability, specialised divergence, balanced versatility, or different combinations under different ecological and social conditions?**

And perhaps the most interesting neurodiversity question is:

> **Can an evolving population maintain multiple distinct cognitive organisations because no single way of perceiving, remembering, deciding, learning, socialising, and acting is optimal across every environment?**

The current simulation is structurally capable of beginning to investigate that question.

One particularly important discovery from the rewrite is that your existing **“Balanced → Specialised → Highly divergent”** axis is already conceptually different from your **“Disadvantaged → Typical → Exceptional”** axis. That makes the system much more suitable for studying diversity without automatically treating unusual organisation as impairment.
