It means your game is much closer to a **system you observe repeatedly** than a game where a player can realistically tick off every possible behaviour.

The crucial distinction is between seeing **all of the types of behaviour** and seeing **all possible behavioural situations**.

Your simulation has 62 canonical action states, including feeding, fleeing, hunting, mating, nursing, social dominance, submission, sparring, migration, thermoregulation, caregiving, group coordination, recovery, communication and others. A determined player could probably see most of these eventually.

But the same action can occur in thousands or millions of different contexts. A `guard` action could involve a father guarding a nursing female, a mother guarding offspring, an animal guarding a carcass, a group member responding to danger, or an individual acting because of a relationship and memory unique to that particular world. Your code explicitly allows many such competing motivations and circumstances.

So there is no meaningful point where you could say:

> “I have now seen every possible behaviour.”

You almost certainly never could.

## How fast simulated time passes

Your current simulation treats an interaction tick as approximately one simulated minute in the ordinary mode. At a maximum rate of about 60 ticks per real second, this gives a useful conversion:

**1 real second ≈ 1 simulated hour**

and therefore:

**24 real seconds ≈ 1 simulated day**

and:

**about 2 hours 26 minutes ≈ 1 simulated year.**

That makes generational observation surprisingly practical.

Your species also have very different life histories. For example, the Meadow Nibbler reaches reproductive maturity at about 20 simulated days, while the Great Plains Grazer reaches maturity at about 130 days and the Armoured Browser at about 155 days. Gestation ranges from around 18 days in the Meadow Nibbler to about 135 days in the Armoured Browser.

At maximum simulation speed, those times become roughly:

- 20 simulated days → **8 real minutes**
    
- 60 days → **24 minutes**
    
- 80 days → **32 minutes**
    
- 130 days → **52 minutes**
    
- 155 days → **62 minutes**
    

So a fast-reproducing species can begin producing a second naturally born generation within tens of real minutes.

That is important because the inheritance system only becomes truly interesting once you start observing descendants rather than the founding population.

## A realistic “I've seen the game” timescale

I would roughly divide it like this.

**After 30–60 minutes**, you should have seen most of the ordinary survival loop: travelling, feeding, drinking, resting, resource searching, threat reactions, group interactions, some predation, communication, physiological stress and ordinary social behaviour. In a sufficiently populated world, many of the 62 action states will already have occurred somewhere.

**After 2–5 hours**, you can reasonably expect to have seen a substantial portion of the reproductive and family systems as well: courtship, rejection, mating, conception, pregnancy, birth, nursing, caregiving, offspring behaviour, social conflicts, changing relationships, deaths and perhaps the beginnings of naturally born animals reaching maturity. Whether particular rare events occur will still depend heavily on species and world conditions.

**After perhaps 10–20 hours across several different worlds**, I would expect a player to have encountered **most major categories of behaviour your simulation presently contains**. That is a better interpretation of “seeing most things.” You would have seen predation, reproduction, caregiving, social hierarchies, resource crises, different species strategies, climatic pressures, learning, memory effects and generational inheritance in various combinations.

But even after 100 hours, the player would still not have “seen everything.”

## Why not?

Because the simulation is not selecting scenes from a finite library.

Consider two females.

They can have different:

- inherited traits;
    
- body sizes;
    
- aggression;
    
- memories;
    
- mating histories;
    
- mate preferences;
    
- relationships;
    
- health;
    
- energy;
    
- fear;
    
- learned experiences.
    

Your Laboratory already exposes many such dimensions on individual animals.

Now place each female in front of three different males.

Those males also have different traits and histories.

Now change:

- the season;
    
- food abundance;
    
- predator proximity;
    
- pregnancy status;
    
- group structure;
    
- previous rejection;
    
- kinship;
    
- resource scarcity.
    

The resulting courtship might still ultimately display the same visible `courtship`, `reject`, or `mating` action, but **the causal story is different**.

You are therefore generating behavioural _episodes_, not just animations.

## Generations make this even more open-ended

Once naturally born animals begin reproducing, the behavioural space becomes even less repeatable.

A newborn receives parent-derived values for size, aggression, scent ability, water ability, food ability, mating ability, care affinity and memory persistence, with bounded offspring variation.

Therefore Generation 2 contains combinations that were not identical to Generation 1.

Generation 3 inherits from Generation 2.

Then:

> selection + reproduction + variation + learning + different histories

creates Generation 4.

So even if you somehow catalogued every animal in one world, a later world or later generation could produce organisms with behavioural configurations you had never previously observed.

This is analogous to asking:

> “How long would I have to watch real animals before I had seen every possible interaction between them?”

There isn't really an answer.

You can see all the **categories**, but not every possible history.

## Neurodivergence makes that especially significant

The inherited system separates **overall capability** from **trait divergence**, so naturally born animals can be balanced, specialised or highly divergent without divergence simply meaning better or worse.

Imagine you watch one highly divergent animal.

It might have:

> unusually strong memory  
> poor food skill  
> low aggression  
> high care affinity.

Later another highly divergent animal might have:

> weak memory  
> exceptional water skill  
> high aggression  
> strong mating skill.

Both receive the same broad label:

> **highly divergent**

but they could behave almost oppositely.

So even seeing “a highly divergent animal” once does not mean you have seen what highly divergent animals can do.

## Species multiply the problem further

The current registry contains **22 species**, ranging from small fast-reproducing herbivores through large grazers, carnivores, scavengers and omnivores, with different life histories and ecological niches.

Some behaviour is biologically unavailable to some species.

A solitary ambush predator will not produce exactly the same social dynamics as a stable herd herbivore.

A colonial defensive species has behaviour that another species may never perform.

A scavenger's resource decisions differ from a pursuit predator's.

So a single world configuration may not even contain all the ecological contexts necessary to expose every mechanic.

You would need **multiple world setups**.

## If you wanted a completion-style estimate

I would use something like this:

|Play/observation time|What you are likely to understand|
|---|---|
|**30 minutes**|Core survival behaviour|
|**1–3 hours**|Most common social, ecological and reproductive behaviour|
|**5–10 hours**|Major systems and rarer interactions become familiar|
|**10–20 hours across several worlds**|Probably most major _types_ of behaviour|
|**50–100+ hours**|Many unusual combinations, lineages and ecological stories|
|**Everything**|**Effectively impossible**|

Those hours are estimates, not guarantees, because rare events depend on the world seed, species mix, population size and environmental conditions.

The interesting thing is that **100 hours would not mean the simulation had run out of content**.

It would more likely mean the player had learned the rules well enough to recognise why two superficially similar situations were actually different.

## For evolution specifically

If you wanted to _watch evolution rather than simply individual behaviour_, I would think in generations rather than hours.

At maximum simulation speed, a long-lived species can pass through a simulated year in roughly 2.4 real hours. Faster-lived species mature much sooner.

So:

**1 generation** shows inheritance.

**3–5 generations** may begin showing lineage differences.

**10+ generations** starts becoming genuinely interesting for trait-distribution changes.

**Dozens of generations across repeated seeds** is where you could begin making serious claims about selection rather than individual luck or genetic/demographic drift.

And your generational audit was specifically designed to warn against interpreting one short run as stable selection.

So the strange result of all this is:

> **A player could probably see nearly every major mechanic in tens of hours, but could never see every behaviour the simulation can produce.**

That is because the game does not contain trillions of pre-written behaviours. It contains a much smaller set of biological rules capable of producing an enormous number of **distinct lives**.

That is the more meaningful interpretation of the huge behavioural-variability number.
