The biggest problem with leaving the year at **120 days** is not simply that the calendar looks unusual. It creates a mismatch between **annual seasonality** and **biological processes that are being represented in literal days**.

Your code currently has animals aging directly in ecological days, pregnancies advancing directly in ecological days, and female fertility cycles also operating in days. At the same time, the season system repeats every 120 days, with each season lasting only 30 days.

That creates several biological distortions.

### 1. Gestation can span an unrealistic number of “years”

Take a deer-like female with a biologically realistic gestation of about 190–200 days.

In a 365-day year, that is about half a year and fits naturally into an autumn-breeding → spring-birth cycle.

In a 120-day year, 198 days equals **1.65 years**.

So a deer could mate in one “year” and give birth almost two seasonal cycles later. The calendar would say multiple winters and summers had passed during one pregnancy, even though the pregnancy duration itself is biologically reasonable.

That is the clearest incompatibility.

### 2. The female reproductive cycle becomes absurd relative to the year

Your Valley Grazer has a 28-day fertility cycle.

In a 120-day year:

**120 ÷ 28 ≈ 4.3 cycles per year**

That means a continuously cycling female could potentially pass through only around four 28-day cycles before the entire year resets.

A real 28-day estrous recurrence sits inside a 365-day annual system, allowing many potential cycles in principle, even when seasonal breeding restricts which months are actually reproductively active.

The problem is especially obvious when you want both:

- a biologically plausible 28-day estrous interval;
    
- and an annual breeding season.
    

The 120-day calendar is too short to accommodate both naturally.

### 3. Seasons are far too short for many reproductive strategies

Each current season is only **30 days**.

That means:

- a 60-day pregnancy spans 2 whole seasons;
    
- a 90-day pregnancy spans 3 seasons;
    
- a 198-day pregnancy spans more than 6 seasons.
    

Real mammals often key reproduction to photoperiod and season on much longer timescales.

For example, the biologically meaningful pattern is often:

**breed in autumn → gestate through winter → birth in spring**

That pattern fundamentally assumes seasons lasting around three months, not one month.

### 4. Seasonal breeding becomes harder to model realistically

Your reproduction drive already depends on `seasonMods[sim.season].breed`.

With 30-day seasons, a breeding window tied to “Autumn” is very short.

If a female misses that window because she:

- is underweight;
    
- is caring for dependent offspring;
    
- is injured;
    
- fails to encounter a suitable male;
    
- is outside her fertile days;
    

then the breeding opportunity disappears extremely quickly.

That can make seasonal reproduction unnaturally brittle.

### 5. Pregnancy and fertility-cycle ratios become distorted

If you leave gestation artificially short to fit inside 120-day years, then gestation becomes too short relative to the estrous cycle.

For the Valley Grazer:

- current gestation = 60 days;
    
- estrous cycle = 28 days.
    

That is only:

**60 ÷ 28 ≈ 2.1 cycles' worth of time.**

A deer-like gestation of ~198 days would instead be around:

**198 ÷ 28 ≈ 7.1 cycle lengths.**

So the current 120-day structure encourages you to compress pregnancy to preserve its relationship with the season, but doing that damages its relationship with reproductive physiology.

You cannot satisfy both at once.

### 6. A 120-day year forces you to choose between two incompatible interpretations

You effectively have to choose:

**Option A — days are literal biological days.**

Then:

- estrous cycles make sense;
    
- gestations can make sense;
    
- digestion and physiology make sense;
    

but:

- the year and seasons are biologically absurdly short.
    

Or:

**Option B — days are compressed calendar days.**

Then:

- seasonal proportions can look reasonable;
    

but:

- a 28-day cycle is no longer really 28 biological days;
    
- gestation-day values stop being literal;
    
- age, dependency and other physiological durations become hard to interpret.
    

That ambiguity is the core design problem.

### 7. Annual migration is too frequent relative to physiology

Your animals can have seasonal migration behaviour keyed to `sim.season`.

With four 30-day seasons, an animal experiences a complete annual migration cycle every 120 days.

If gestation, dependency, memory, development and body condition are measured in literal days, seasonal environmental change is happening about three times faster than it should relative to those processes.

A 90-day-old juvenile has already experienced three seasons.

A 180-day-old animal has experienced six seasons.

That is ecologically unusual for most of the mammalian analogues.

### 8. Plant and animal phenology become decoupled

Your vegetation has slow ecological processes measured in days and month-like 30-day intervals.

Meanwhile, “winter” arrives every 120 days.

If plant maturation, woody succession, animal pregnancy and age use literal days, seasonal recurrence happens disproportionately quickly.

This can cause things like:

- repeated winters before slow-growing organisms have developed much;
    
- multiple seasonal resource crashes during one pregnancy;
    
- migration cycles occurring much faster than demographic turnover.
    

### 9. Generation time becomes awkward to interpret

Suppose an animal matures at 365 days.

Under a normal calendar:

> maturity at 1 year.

Under your current calendar:

> maturity after 3.04 simulation years.

That makes scientific interpretation unintuitive.

Likewise:

- 730 days = 6.08 current years;
    
- 1,095 days = 9.1 current years.
    

So if you ever start using realistic maturity ages, the displayed “years” stop meaning anything biologically useful.

### 10. Lifespan reporting becomes misleading

Your inspector explicitly reports animal age in days.

That saves you somewhat, because the raw day value is still visible.

But if the world says:

> Year 8

while an animal is only 960 days old,

the player may naturally interpret that as an eight-year-old world, when in real-day terms it is only about 2.6 years.

That becomes especially confusing once you start comparing your simulation with scientific literature.

### 11. Evolutionary rates become harder to communicate

Your theory work relies heavily on generations, selection, reproduction and inherited variation.

If one simulation year is 120 days, then statements such as:

> “this trait became more common over 20 years”

would mean only:

**2,400 days ≈ 6.6 real-calendar years**

of biological time.

That makes evolutionary results harder to compare with real organisms and real ecological timescales.

### 12. Seasonal selection pressure is artificially frequent

A population currently experiences:

- one winter every 120 days;
    
- one drought-season equivalent every 120 days;
    
- one seasonal breeding cycle every 120 days.
    

With a 365-day year, those pressures occur at normal annual frequency.

Leaving it at 120 means seasonal selective pressures recur about:

**365 / 120 ≈ 3.04 times more frequently**

relative to literal-day physiology.

That can materially alter which traits are favoured.

An animal adapted to survive one severe winter every 365 days is facing about three winters in that same biological timespan in the current model.

That's not just cosmetic. It changes selection.

### 13. Pregnancy could encounter too many seasonal transitions

This could alter maternal viability as well.

Your pregnancy system samples maternal condition continuously and accumulates viability loss under poor condition.

If seasons cycle three times faster than physiological time, a pregnancy may encounter:

- winter scarcity;
    
- spring recovery;
    
- summer heat;
    
- autumn scarcity;
    

far more frequently than its real analogue would.

That can alter miscarriage and prenatal-condition outcomes.

### 14. Offspring development is exposed to too many “years”

A dependent offspring with a 180-day dependency period would experience:

**1.5 current years**

before independence.

A real 180-day dependency is simply six months.

Again, the raw days are fine, but the seasonal world around the offspring is moving too quickly.

---

## What is _not_ inherently wrong with 120 days

A 120-day year would be perfectly acceptable if the planet itself were intentionally fictional and had:

- a 120-day orbital period;
    
- 30-day seasons;
    
- animal physiology evolved for that world.
    

Then your animals should also have reproductive systems evolved around that calendar.

For example, a 28-day estrous cycle might plausibly be unusually long relative to such a short year.

But that is **not what your species appear to be based on**. They are deliberately Earth-like ecological analogues—deer, wolves, foxes, large cats, bison-like grazers, bear-like omnivores, and so on.

For Earth-like biology, the 120-day year creates a structural mismatch.

## The central problem in one sentence

The problem is:

> **your physiology operates mainly in literal hours and days, while your seasons operate on a year that is only one-third of an Earth year.**

That means:

**physiology and ecology are running on different implicit clocks.**

For a simulation whose main goal were gameplay abstraction, that might be acceptable.

For one intended to preserve biological causal realism, it is much harder to justify.

So if the reproductive cycle–gestation relationship is your main concern, I would regard **365 days as the cleaner foundation**. You can still compress those 365 ecological days into the same three real hours of observation; the player does not have to wait any longer. What changes is that “28 days,” “198 days,” “one season,” and “one year” can all mean biologically coherent things at the same time.
